After a few interviews the details blur together: one candidate was personable, another gave sharp answers, a third mentioned testing at some point. An interview scorecard exists so that the hiring decision rests on written evidence rather than on that blur. Below: what a good scorecard contains, how to build one in an hour, and how to use it so it actually changes decisions.

What an interview scorecard is

A scorecard is a one-page form that every interviewer fills in about every candidate, using the same rules. It has five parts:

  1. Criteria derived from the role: four to six, no more.
  2. Weights showing which criteria matter more for this particular job.
  3. A scale with anchors: what exactly a 1, a 3 and a 5 mean for each criterion.
  4. Evidence: a quote or note from the interview that the score is based on.
  5. An overall score and recommendation, entered before the interviewer sees anyone else's opinion.

The case for structure is empirical. In the meta-analysis by Sackett and colleagues (2022), structured interviews had the highest mean operational validity (r = .42) among the selection methods analysed, although individual studies varied widely. A scorecard does not guarantee that result, but it gives you the structure in which such interviews can be run and compared.

How to build one in seven steps

1. Start from outcomes, not traits

Write down what this person must deliver in their first 90 days. Criteria should test the ability to deliver those outcomes. "Positive attitude" measures nothing; "can take an ambiguous task to production without hand-holding" does.

2. Pick four to six criteria

Ten criteria mean none will be assessed properly in a 45-minute interview. If the list grows, merge related items or move some to another stage.

3. Assign weights

Use ×3 for a critical criterion and ×1 for a supporting one. Set weights before the first interview. Changing them after you have met the candidates turns the scorecard into a justification of earlier preferences.

4. Describe the scale

A 1–5 scale works only if each point describes behaviour rather than an adjective. "Independent" is an adjective; "clarified the requirements on their own and proposed a solution, but did not discuss alternatives" is a behaviour you can check against the answer.

5. Add a field for evidence

One or two sentences next to every score: what the candidate said or did. A score without evidence is an opinion.

6. Decide who scores and when

At least two interviewers. Each fills in the scorecard right after the interview and does not read the others until their own is submitted.

7. Plan the debrief

Start with the criteria where the scores diverge. That is usually where the most information is.

How to use it so it changes decisions

  • Score independently. The first opinion spoken aloud anchors everyone else. Collect scorecards before anyone speaks.
  • Treat disagreement as a signal. A two-point gap on the same criterion means the interviewers saw different things or read the scale differently. Go back to the evidence: a transcript, a note, the code from the exercise.
  • Do not average blindly. An average hides that one person gave a 4 and another a 2. Settle who is right first, then compute the score.
  • Set a threshold in advance. For example: an offer only at a weighted score of 3.5 or higher and no key criterion below 3. Without a threshold there is always a reason to hire someone we like.
  • Calibrate interviewers. Some score generously, others strictly. After a few hires, compare each person's average and agree on how to read the scale.

Common mistakes

  • "Culture fit" with no definition. Replace it with behaviours: how the candidate describes a conflict in a team, how they react to criticism, how they decide with incomplete data.
  • Too many criteria. The result is scores around three everywhere.
  • The halo effect. A brilliant first answer lifts every later score. Fill in the scorecard right after the interview, criterion by criterion, and score the evidence rather than the overall impression.
  • Changing criteria mid-process. If the scorecard needs fixing, fix it and re-score all candidates.
  • No evidence. "I think she is independent" is not a score.

A note on candidate data

Scores and notes about a candidate are personal data under the GDPR. Write them as if the candidate might one day read them, keep them factual, and delete them when the recruitment ends unless the candidate has agreed to be considered for future roles. If an AI tool proposes scores, make sure a person reviews and owns every decision.
